# Root of unity

An *n*th [root of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_unity) is a solution of `zⁿ = 1`:
the *n* points `ζ⁰, ζ¹, …, ζⁿ⁻¹` spaced evenly around the unit circle, where `ζ = e^(2πi/n)`.
They are the algebraic form of *a thing that wraps* — a heading that turns by a fixed angle, a
socket on a ring, a residue modulo a prime. The reason they keep appearing in the problems below
is not that any of them is about complex numbers. It is that each one asks a question about a
cyclic structure, and roots of unity turn that question into polynomial arithmetic with exact
integer answers.

## The idea

The *n*th roots of unity form a cyclic group of order *n* under multiplication, generated by any
`ζᵏ` with `gcd(k, n) = 1` — the [primitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_unity#Definition)
ones, of which there are `φ(n)`. That group is the same abstract object as `ℤ/nℤ` under addition,
which is why "socket *k* of *n*" and "`ζᵏ`" are interchangeable descriptions of the same thing.

What makes them computationally useful is that we know *exactly* which linear relations hold
between them. Over the rationals, the minimal polynomial of a primitive *n*th root is the
[cyclotomic polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclotomic_polynomial) `Φₙ`, of degree
`φ(n)` and with integer coefficients, and

```
xⁿ - 1 = ∏_{d | n} Φ_d(x)
```

For a prime *p*, `Φ_p = 1 + x + … + x^(p-1)`, which says the *p* distinct *p*th roots sum to zero
and — crucially — that this is the *only* relation among them. So a question of the form "does
this sum of roots of unity vanish?" is never a numerical question. Encode a subset
`S ⊆ {0, …, n-1}` as the 0/1 polynomial `f_S(x) = Σ_{k∈S} xᵏ`; then

```
Σ_{k∈S} ζᵏ = 0   ⟺   Φₙ(x) divides f_S(x)
```

That equivalence is the whole subject in one line. Geometry on the left, exact integer algebra on
the right.

## Vanishing sums

Problem 208 is the cleanest instance. A robot walks 72° arcs, choosing left or right each step, and
must return to where it started. Because every turn rotates the heading by ±72°, only five
directions ever occur, and they are the fifth roots of unity. Their only rational relation is
`v₀ + v₁ + v₂ + v₃ + v₄ = 0`, so a non-negative integer combination `Σ mᵢvᵢ` vanishes **if and only
if all five multiplicities are equal**. The closure condition — a statement about a position in the
plane — collapses to "each of the five directions is used the same number of times", which is a
counting problem a [dynamic program](/topics/technique/dynamic-programming) can walk directly. Note
what was bought: no coordinates, no floating point, and an immediate corollary that the arc count
must be a multiple of five.

Problem 768 is the same equivalence at the other end of the difficulty range. Candles in a ring of
*n* sockets balance exactly when their unit vectors cancel, so `f(n, m)` counts the *m*-subsets with
`Φₙ | f_S`. The obvious guess is that a balanced set must be a disjoint union of regular *p*-gons
`{j, j + n/p, …}` for primes `p | n`. That guess is a **theorem** (Lam and Leung) when *n* has at
most two distinct prime factors — and false as soon as it has three. In `ℤ₃₀` there are balanced
6-subsets containing no complete polygon at all: the four non-trivial fifth roots sum to −1, the
negatives of the two non-trivial cube roots sum to +1, and the six cancel. Any method that pattern-
matches on polygons silently undercounts; a method that asks the algebraic question gets the exotic
configurations for free.

Two structural facts make the algebraic question cheap, and both are worth knowing on their own:

- **Layering.** With `r = rad(n)` the [radical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_of_an_integer)
  and `t = n/r`, we have `Φₙ(x) = Φ_r(xᵗ)`. Splitting the sockets by residue mod *t* therefore
  decomposes any balanced arrangement into *t* independent balanced subsets of `ℤ_r`. The problem
  stops depending on *n* except through its radical, and the *t* layers combine by raising one
  [generating function](/topics/domain/generating-function) to the *t*th power.
- **Exact coordinates.** `ℤ[ζ_r]` is free of rank `φ(r)`, so each socket is a small integer vector
  and "balanced" is "the vectors sum to the zero vector". The basis relation on each prime axis is
  `ζ_p^(p-1) = -(1 + ζ_p + … + ζ_p^(p-2))`, i.e. the last residue maps to the all-`−1` vector. This
  is an exact test with none of the tolerance guesswork a floating-point sum of unit vectors would
  need.

There is a neat packing trick on top of the second point. If a subset has at most *r* elements,
every coordinate of its sum stays in `[-r, r]`, so choosing a base `B > 2r` lets you store the whole
`φ(r)`-vector as a *single* integer with no carries between coordinates. Vector addition becomes
integer addition, and the balance test becomes `== 0`.

## Roots of unity modulo p

The same group turns up in a completely different setting, and recognising it is worth more than
any individual trick. The multiplicative group `(ℤ/pℤ)*` is
[cyclic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_group_of_integers_modulo_n) of order `p - 1`,
so the solutions of `uʳ = 1 (mod p)` are the unique subgroup of order `gcd(r, p-1)` — there are
exactly that many, never more, never fewer.

Problem 421 runs on that count. Asked for the prime factors of `n¹⁵ + 1`, you exchange the order of
summation and ask, per prime, how many `n ≤ N` satisfy `n¹⁵ ≡ -1`. Since `(-1)¹⁵ = -1`, those *n*
are precisely the negatives of the 15th roots of unity mod *p*, so their number is
`d = gcd(15, p-1) ∈ {1, 3, 5, 15}` — decided by two cheap congruence tests, `p ≡ 1 (mod 3)` and
`p ≡ 1 (mod 5)`. The fact that *d* is never zero is exactly why `n¹⁵ + 1` is composite for every
`n > 1` in the first place. A factorisation problem becomes a residue-class census.

Producing the roots is textbook and worth having in your fingers:

```python
g = pow(a, (p - 1) // r, p)   # g^r == 1 by Fermat, for any base a
                              # g == 1 means a was an r-th power — retry with the next a
roots = [pow(g, i, p) for i in range(r)]
```

A share `(r-1)/r` of bases work, so `a = 2` almost always suffices. And because a cyclic group of
order 15 is the internal direct product of its subgroups of orders 3 and 5, the 15th roots need no
exponentiation of their own — they are the products `ω^i τ^j` of the cube roots and fifth roots.

## The roots-of-unity filter

One more use, and the one most engineers meet first. Summing a function over all *n*th roots of
unity annihilates everything whose index is not a multiple of *n*, because `Σ_j ζ^(jk)` is *n* when
`n | k` and 0 otherwise. That gives the
[filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_unity#Summation)

```
Σ_{n | k} a_k = (1/n) · Σ_{j=0}^{n-1} A(ζʲ)
```

for a generating function `A`. It is the finite-group version of "take only every *n*th term", it is
the reason [FFT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fast_Fourier_transform) works, and it is the standard
way to answer "how many of these have total ≡ 0 mod *n*" without an *n*-slot table.

## How to reason about it

- **The tell is a cyclic structure**, not a complex number: a heading that turns by a fixed angle, a
  position on a ring, an exponent taken mod something, or a count you need modulo *n*. If the object
  wraps, `ℤ/nℤ` is in play and its character group is the roots of unity.
- **Never sum unit vectors in floating point.** The whole value of the subject is that vanishing is
  decidable exactly. Work in `ℤ[ζ]` coordinates, or with `Φₙ`-divisibility, or in `ℤ[x]/(xⁿ - 1)`.
  A near-zero double tells you nothing, and the exotic cancellations are precisely where it will
  mislead you.
- **Know which relations exist.** For prime *n*, only the all-roots sum. For composite *n*, `Φₙ` is
  the complete answer and the polygon intuition is a *sufficient* condition masquerading as a
  necessary one — safe for at most two distinct primes, wrong beyond that.
- **Reduce to the radical.** `Φₙ(x) = Φ_r(xᵗ)` means the hard work only ever scales with
  `rad(n)`, and repeated prime factors cost you *t* independent copies of a smaller problem rather
  than a bigger one.
- **Mod *p*, the count is `gcd(r, p-1)`.** No search, no factorisation of `p - 1`, and the generator
  is one modular exponentiation away.
